Industry Structure

Status: Tool released for public review

How the mass timber industry is organized, who holds bargaining power within it, and what that means for the flow of capital into it — per Michael Porter's Five Forces approach.

Author: Noel A. Johnson, Conrad / Old i, LLC
Published: November 2026, Mass Timber Finance & Consumer

Why map an industry

Industries operating within a global capitalist regime share common elements. This page incorporates this by offering a stylized depiction of the mass timber ecosystem. Built to illuminate, organize and spur productive discussion, it distills an assessment of the industry's attractiveness (profit potential), its gaps (the opportunity nodes) and its business-plan dependencies. Scenarios offer the user insights across drivers and controllable versus uncontrollable forces relative to market demand and constraints.

A distinction worth holding onto.

  • Scaling the market is not the same as growing demand. Fixing bottlenecks, improving capital access and building firm capacity release or scale the market that already exists.
  • Demand growth is separate. It is organic growth in what buyers want, as distinct from pent-up demand finally being met.
  • Total addressable market (“TAM”) is a demand-side notion: what is bought today plus what could be tomorrow.
  • For mass timber, TAM can be estimated in a wide band, because the product substitutes for structural means already used across market and non-market real estate development.

The framework, adapted

Porter's Five Forces, first described by Harvard's Michael Porter in 1979, is a foundational insight of business strategy: widely taught, it shapes how firms and academics think, which makes it familiar and accepted. There are more well developed, advanced frameworks; but starting from a simple, more universally known notion has merit of its own. The idea is to show how an industry's economic value (however large it is today, or might be tomorrow) is divided among the parties that touch it. The spirit at the micro level is to help firms figure out how to position themselves and their products. At the macro level, trends and potential impacts can be understood and anticipated.

What acts on the forces

Porter is explicit that government is not a sixth force: it matters through its effect on the five. The same holds for finance and for the industry's own choices. Finance is largely agnostic; where it weighs on the industry, the cause is missing information on risk and how to underwrite it, or missing products such as hedging and insurance, given how new the industry is.

Forces characterization

Each force node implies a level of bargaining power as parties work to claim profits on offer. Porter's central point is that stronger forces can shift away the value an industry creates. Value from general-purpose technological innovations often ends up being claimed by consumers, not the firms. This is much debated across the AI ecosystem: how much of the total value created will end up with Nvidia, Anthropic, Microsoft, Digital Realty Trust, power generators or the citizen at their own computer. Be it AI or mass timber, four implications inform capital’s flow into an ecosystem.

Value created may not be value captured

Demand creates value, but whoever holds power may capture it: the manufacturer, the development team that builds the asset, or the saver and investor who own it over time.

Pull usually beats push

Buyers asking for the product is the growth to underwrite. New capacity can release pent-up demand, but it does not show how much demand exists or whether it is growing.

Only some risks are controllable

The industry can move buyers through marketing, suppliers through collaboration and regulators through engagement. It cannot set consumer preferences, carbon policy, tariffs or interest rates.

The map moves

Codes, taxes and the cost of capital change, and new plants open. A strategy built to respond quickly to new signals is the purpose of the scenarios in Part C.

The baseline: the author's assessment

The map shows the author's baseline reading of the industry, drawn from his development practice, research and industry engagement. The industry's own product firms circle the centre, where rivalry and collaboration among them play out. Supply inputs orbit on the left and demand on the right; concrete and steel, and the firms able to enter, circle the whole. The brighter a body's halo and the heavier its stream toward the centre, the more bargaining power it holds over the industry's firms. How to read the map

Five bodies, five levels of power. The levels are offered for comment, not as settled findings; Part C is where you argue with them.

Force by force

Each level is the share of Porter's tests for that force which mass timber meets, on a scale of 0 to 100. Source: Michael E. Porter, “The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy,” Harvard Business Review, January 2008.

Rivalry / collaborationMedium · 67
Among the firms that create mass timber products and buildings · 6.5 of 9 tests met, less ½ for collaboration under way
  • Many small producers of roughly similar size compete for the same projects.
  • Plants are capital-heavy, specialised and added in large steps, and the sector runs well below installed capacity: the conditions under which price competition is hardest.
  • Most firms entered the industry on forward expectations and passion, and are strongly committed. As firms recapitalise after bankruptcy or merge, ownership is becoming a mix of dedicated firms and more dispassionate, diversified owners.
  • Products are only partly standardised: many buyers do not distinguish one producer's panel from another's, though species, strength, appearance and quality assurance do differ.
  • The product is not perishable, but it is often custom to a project: if an order falls through, it is not easily resold.
  • Integrated forest owners can take their return in the timber or the mill, and so can price products near cost.
  • The industry is growing, which eases the fight for share, and firms already collaborate on standards, testing and shared promotion. The author deducts half a point for that collaboration; this adjustment is his, not one of Porter's tests.
Power of buyersMedium · 56
Occupants, investors, civic and political buyers · 4.5 of 8 tests met
  • There are many developers, investors and owners. Even large orders from hyperscalers, or long commitments such as a corporate campus, are well under a quarter of a year's industry volume.
  • Products are not fully standardised: wood species, strength and visual character differ, as do each firm's manufacturing process and quality assurance.
  • Switching costs are low; bids are compared project by project.
  • Structure is an easily seen and closely watched cost, but it is often smaller than the exterior envelope or the mechanical, electrical and plumbing decisions, and construction is itself 50 to 75 percent of a project's total cost. It matters less arithmetically than commonly perceived.
  • Development and ownership returns are thin and financed, which makes buyers price-sensitive.
  • Whether the structure changes the quality of what the buyer offers is poorly understood. Emerging research, such as the apartment renters' willingness-to-pay study, suggests it does.
Power of suppliersHigh · 75
Wood fiber, mills, lumber; adhesives, energy, equipment, land, labor · 4.5 of 6 tests met
  • Mass timber is a very small share of lumber demand, so fiber suppliers do not depend on it.
  • There is no substitute for wood fiber.
    Expand for context: mass timber’s scale against concrete

    In 2024 the North American mass timber sector consumed an estimated 187 million board feet of lumber, about the annual output of one large modern sawmill, and its panel plants ran at under 40 percent of capacity (R. Anderson, The Beck Group, and E. Spiritos, Mass Timber Performance Index, International Mass Timber Report, 2025). U.S. cement shipments alone were about 100 million tons worth $17 billion in 2025 (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026), before counting the concrete, reinforcing and structural steel built with and around it. The incumbent the industry is measured against is larger by well over an order of magnitude. A fuller estimate of the mass timber industry's scale is a subject of the author's further research.

  • Forest and sawmill owners commonly enter mass timber production, and standing timber gives them the option to wait. General contractors such as Swinerton are integrating the other way, into fabrication, production and mills.
  • Lumber and energy are commodities, but presses, CNC equipment and certified adhesive systems are partly differentiated. Species, grade and moisture specifications narrow the choice of supplier moderately.
  • Lumber supply is fragmented; adhesives and equipment are more concentrated.
Power of substitutesHigh · 88
Concrete and steel (mostly concrete) · 1.75 of 2 tests met
  • Concrete and steel are familiar, accepted and often cheaper, though cost evidence is mixed and project-specific.
  • The extent to which their negative externalities are valued sharply informs the price-performance trade-off. Today those externalities are largely unpriced.
  • Choosing them costs a buyer nothing: they are the default design.
  • Porter gives only two tests for this force, so this level rests more on judgment than the others.
Threat of new entrantsMedium · 50
Integrated forest owners, engineered wood producers, concrete and steel firms, large contractors · 4 of 8 barriers present
  • Capital requirements are substantial.
  • Know-how, certification and project record loom large: if the product fails in delivery, quality or specification detail, the ripple effects on the wider development are large.
  • Scale favours incumbents: plants benefit from volume, and producers with several regional plants can balance work across them, which a single-plant newcomer cannot.
  • Product certifications are hard to obtain, and local code reviewers often ask for proof beyond their explicit mandate because the product is new.
  • Customers face no switching costs, distribution is open, and incumbents are small, under-used and have not retaliated against entrants so far.

What the baseline means for capital flows

If value is created by end-user consumption, and captured by whoever holds comparatively more bargaining power to retain it, then today's substitutes and the industry's own rivalry appear to be the primary points of control. Thus, mass timber industry firms face challenges retaining the value created by their product. Two of the forces are ones the industry can move itself: buyers' power, through bolstering evidence of consumer and investor value (i.e. marketing-related investments), and rivalry, through collaboration on standards, testing, shared learning and risk reduction. The power of substitutes appears to be driven by carbon, tariff and energy policies or de-globalization trends: factors that are not specific to the industry. Regulation can work in either direction. How much of the wood may be left visible, and so how much consumer value the product can show, is set by building codes that differ widely. In the United States, 38 states have adopted the tall mass timber provisions of the 2021 or 2024 International Building Code; the 2024 edition allows fully exposed timber ceilings in buildings of up to twelve storeys, and full exposure up to about eight (WoodWorks, status as of April 2026). In Europe the rules vary country by country: a 2020 review found that some nations, including Germany and the United Kingdom, did not then permit untreated visible wood inside taller residential buildings, and Sweden only to about two storeys, while others, including Austria, Finland and Switzerland, allowed it widely (Östman, National fire regulations for the use of wood in buildings: worldwide review 2020, Linnaeus University).

Propose a scenario

Force levels are the author's baseline assessment, scored against Porter's tests on a 0–100 scale; scenario shifts are proposed directions and relative scales, not measurements, and every scenario can reach every force. Attractiveness follows Porter's statement that “the strongest competitive force or forces determine the profitability of an industry”: it is 100 minus half the strongest force's level and one-eighth of each of the other four. Porter gives no formula; this rule is the author's.
