Industry Structure

Part A

Why map an industry

Industries operating within global capitalism share common elements. This page is a distilled, stylized depiction of the mass timber ecosystem, built to illuminate and organize it. The goal is to be digestible and organizing: to help firms assess the industry's attractiveness (for profits), its competition (and its collaboration) and its gaps (the opportunity nodes), and to encourage clarity about business-plan dependencies — pull versus push dollar drivers, controllable versus uncontrollable risks — by separating the market's demand drivers from its supply constraints.

A distinction worth holding onto. Fixing bottlenecks, improving capital access, building firm capacity and improving forecasting can unleash the total market, but that is not market growth driven by demand; it allows existing, pent-up demand to be realized. Total addressable market is a demand-side notion: what is bought today plus what could be tomorrow. For mass timber it can reasonably be estimated, in a wide band, because the product is a substitute for existing means used across market and non-market real estate development.

The framework, adapted

Porter's Five Forces, first described by Harvard's Michael Porter in 1979, is a core insight of business strategy: widely taught, it shapes how firms and academics think, which makes it familiar and accepted. It shows how an industry's economic value is divided among the parties that touch it, and so how a company can position itself. Applied to mass timber, the five forces become five bodies.

  • Supply from firms — producers of CLT, glulam and mass plywood, fabricators, the fiber chain behind them (forestry, mills, lumber) and the general inputs they all buy (adhesives, tooling, plant real estate, energy).
  • Demand from buyers — investors, end users and political buyers. End users include residents of townhomes and apartments, employees at the office, students and teachers in classrooms, and families inside a public library.
  • Substitutes — the traditional means: concrete and steel.
  • Future entrants — novel building means: low-carbon concrete and steel, plastics and polymers, and systems not yet in the codes.
  • Shared, central forces — rivalries and collaborations, concentrations, regulator impacts and economic trends, which act on everyone at once.

What the forces imply

Each force carries a level of bargaining power. Porter's central point is that the stronger the forces, the less of the value an industry creates that its own firms get to keep. Four implications follow for anyone deciding whether, and how, to put capital into mass timber.

Value created is not value captured

Demand creates the value; whoever holds power captures it. Buyer power and substitute power decide how much of mass timber's value stays with mass timber firms.

Pull beats push

Demand-driven growth (buyers asking for the product) is the growth to underwrite. Capacity-driven growth releases pent-up demand that already exists, and should be read as such.

Some risks are yours to control

The industry can move buyers' power with evidence and suppliers' power with collaboration. It cannot set carbon policy, tariffs or interest rates; those it anticipates and positions for.

The map moves

Forces are not fixed. A code change, a tax, a new plant or a rate cut shifts the balance, and with it where capital is comfortable going. Part C lets you move them yourself.

How to read the map

Five bodies, five levels of power. The levels are offered for comment, not as settled findings; Part C is where you argue with them.

Force by force

Power of buyersHigh
Demand side: investors, end users and political buyers
  • The demand side is collectively much larger than the suppliers serving it.
  • Products are comparatively undifferentiated, yet a material cost consideration to buyers.
  • Switching costs across suppliers are relatively low, and substitutes are easily accepted.
  • The product's value proposition is not understood, and often confused. The desire to purchase is driven by many buyer typologies — residents, firm CEOs and politicians opt for wood environments for many reasons — and taxonometric, comparative-value research is a needed, nascent realm.
  • Buyer power informs where value is captured, which is not the same as where it is created.
Power of suppliersMedium
Supply side: product-specific firms, wood inputs and general inputs
  • Supplier power — the ability to secure better prices or terms — varies by distinct sub-group. Key factors are concentrations, dependencies, alternatives and time-based considerations.
  • General inputs (energy, adhesives, labor, real estate): strong power.
  • Wood inputs (fiber, mills, lumber): medium power.
  • Product-specific firms (factories, fabricators, on-site): low power.
  • Supply dynamics suggest value capture is not evenly shared, absent non-market forces such as collaboration or regulation.
Power of substitutesHigh
Traditional means: concrete and steel structures
  • Attractive pricing.
  • Risks are known and accepted.
  • Buyers switch to them, and use them, easily.
Power of future entrantsLow
Novel building means: low-carbon concrete and steel, plastics and polymers
  • Unattractive costs.
  • Unknown risks.
  • Buyers are not easily able to switch to them and use them.
Shared, central forcesRivalry: TBD · Regulators and economy: High
Rivalries, collaborations, concentrations, regulator impacts and economic trends
  • Rivalry and collaboration: while traditional means are highly centralized, mass timber's supply and demand forces are new; competition, partnership and merger activity is fast and ongoing.
  • Regulators: building codes, tariffs and similar actions have increasingly high impacts.
  • Economy: economic and real estate cycles are hugely impactful.

What the baseline means for capital flows

Value is created on the demand side and captured by whoever holds the power. Today that is buyers and substitutes, so the industry's own firms keep less of the value they create than the industry's growth story implies. Two of the forces are ones the industry can move itself: buyers' power, through evidence of consumer and investor value (the Underwriting Mass Timber Apartments and Listening to Investors work), and suppliers' power, through collaboration mechanisms such as cost stabilization. Two it cannot: the pricing of substitutes, which turns on carbon policy, tariffs and energy, and the regulatory and economic cycle. Those are to be anticipated and positioned for.
