Capital Markets / Entrepreneurship

Industry Structure

Status: Working edition · Request for comment

How the mass timber industry is organized, who holds bargaining power within it, and what that means for the flow of capital into it — per Michael Porter's Five Forces approach.

Author: Noel A. Johnson, Conrad / Old i, LLC
Published: November 2026, Mass Timber Finance & Consumer

Why map an industry

Industries operating within a global capitalist regime share common elements. This page incorporates this by offering a stylized depiction of the mass timber ecosystem. Built to illuminate, organize and spur productive discussion, it distills an assessment of the industry's attractiveness (profit potential), its gaps (the opportunity nodes) and its business-plan dependencies. Scenarios offer the user insights across drivers and controllable versus uncontrollable forces relative to market demand and constraints.

A distinction worth holding onto.

  • Scaling the market is not the same as growing demand. Fixing bottlenecks, improving capital access and building firm capacity release or scale the market that already exists.
  • Demand growth is separate. It is organic growth in what buyers want, as distinct from pent-up demand finally being met.
  • Total addressable market (“TAM”) is a demand-side notion: what is bought today plus what could be tomorrow.
  • For mass timber, TAM can be estimated in a wide band, because the product substitutes for structural means already used across market and non-market real estate development.

The framework, adapted

Porter's Five Forces, first described by Harvard's Michael Porter in 1979, is a foundational insight of business strategy: widely taught, it shapes how firms and academics think, which makes it familiar and accepted. There are more well developed, advanced frameworks; but starting from a simple, more universally known notion has merit of its own. The idea is to show how an industry's economic value (however large it is today, or might be tomorrow) is divided among the parties that touch it. The spirit at the micro level is to help firms figure out how to position themselves and their products. At the macro level, trends and potential impacts can be understood and anticipated.

  • Supply from firms — producers of CLT, glulam and mass plywood, fabricators, the fiber chain behind them (forestry, mills, lumber) and the general inputs they all require (adhesives, tooling, real estate, energy).
  • Demand from buyers — investors, occupants and/or governments. End users include residents of townhomes and apartments, employees at the office, students and teachers in classrooms, and families inside a public library. They also include those with savings via pensions, insurance policies, etc. who use real estate to safeguard and grow their retained earnings. And finally, politicians who use buildings for political signal and symbolism.
  • Substitutes — the traditional structural solutions of concrete and steel.
  • Future entrants — novel building means: low-carbon concrete or steel, plastics and polymers, and systems not yet in the codes… (fun examples include paper, as demonstrated by Shigeru Ban.)
  • Common, central forces — rivalries and collaborations, concentrations, regulator impacts and economic trends, which act on everyone at once. From firms to geographies to nations, the nature of generative versus degrading competition explain trends and possibilities. Network effects, agglomeration economies and demand nodes supported by wealth concentrations provide examples of how concentrations inform and influence. Regulatory impacts are well known, from local city building officials to global carbon policies. And exogenous economic shocks – including those from energy spikes, tariffs impacts or immigration constraints to labor supply are well demonstrated today.

Forces characterization

Each force node implies a level of bargaining power as parties work to claim profits on offer. Porter's central point is that stronger forces can shift away the value an industry creates. Value from general-purpose technological innovations often ends up being claimed by consumers, not the firms. This is much debated across the AI ecosystem: how much of the total value created will end up with Nvidia, Anthropic, Microsoft, Digital Realty Trust, power generators or the citizen at their own computer. Be it AI or mass timber, four implications inform capital’s flow into an ecosystem.

Value created may not be value captured

Demand creates value, but whoever holds power may capture it: the manufacturer, the development team that builds the asset, or the saver and investor who own it over time.

Pull usually beats push

Buyers asking for the product is the growth to underwrite. New capacity can release pent-up demand, but it does not show how much demand exists or whether it is growing.

Only some risks are controllable

The industry can move buyers through marketing, suppliers through collaboration and regulators through engagement. It cannot set consumer preferences, carbon policy, tariffs or interest rates.

The map moves

Codes, taxes and the cost of capital change, and new plants open. A strategy built to respond quickly to new signals is the purpose of the scenarios in Part C.

How to read the map

Five bodies, five levels of power. The levels are offered for comment, not as settled findings; Part C is where you argue with them.

Force by force

Power of buyersHigh
Demand side: investors (savers), occupants (firms & families) and government (political)
  • The demand side is collectively much larger than the suppliers serving it.
  • Products are comparatively undifferentiated, yet a material cost consideration to buyers.
  • Switching costs across suppliers are relatively low, and substitutes are easily accepted.
  • The product's value proposition is not understood, and often confused. The desire to purchase is driven by many buyer typologies — residents, firm CEOs and politicians opt for wood environments for many reasons.
Power of suppliersMedium
Supply side: product-specific firms, wood inputs and general inputs
  • Supplier power (the ability to secure better prices or terms) varies by distinct sub-group. Key factors are concentrations of supply-chain execution (which colors dependency risks) as well as alternatives and time-based considerations.
  • General inputs (energy, adhesives, labor, real estate): strong power.
  • Wood inputs (fiber, mills, lumber): high power. If wood fiber demand from elsewhere grows, mass timber products have zero substitute.
    Expand for context: mass timber’s scale against concrete

    In 2024 the North American mass timber sector consumed an estimated 187 million board feet of lumber, about the annual output of one large modern sawmill, and its panel plants ran at under 40 percent of capacity (International Mass Timber Report, 2025). At reported panel prices that is very roughly half a billion dollars of product a year. U.S. cement shipments alone were about 100 million tons worth $17 billion in 2025 (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026), before counting the concrete, reinforcing and structural steel built with and around it. The incumbent the industry is measured against is larger by well over an order of magnitude.

  • Product-specific firms (factories, fabricators, on-site): low power; few buyers differentiate which CLT panel is in their building.
  • Supply dynamics suggest value capture is easily retained; however, non-market forces such as collaboration or regulation impact this.
Power of substitutesHigh
Traditional means: concrete and steel structures
  • Attractive pricing and execution.
  • Risks are known and accepted.
  • Buyers accept/use them without thought.
Power of future entrantsLow
Novel building means: low-carbon concrete and steel, plastics and polymers
  • Unattractive costs.
  • Unknown risks.
  • Buyers are not easily able to switch to them and use them. High regulatory barriers exist.
Shared, central forcesRivalry: TBD · Regulators and economy: High
Rivalries, collaborations, concentrations, regulator impacts and economic trends
  • Rivalry and collaboration: while traditional means are highly centralized, mass timber's supply and demand forces are new; competition, partnership and merger activity is fast and ongoing.
  • Regulators: building codes, tariffs, immigration and energy policies and similar actions have increasingly high impacts.
  • Economy: economic and real estate cycles are hugely impactful.

What the baseline means for capital flows

If value is created by end-user consumption, and captured by whoever holds comparatively more bargaining power to retain it, then today's buyers and substitutes appear to be the primary points of control. Thus, mass timber industry firms face challenges retaining the value created by their product. Two of the forces are ones the industry can move itself: buyers' power, through bolstering evidence of consumer and investor value (i.e. marketing-related investments), and suppliers' power, through collaboration mechanisms such as cost stabilization and risk reduction. The power of substitutes appears to be driven by carbon, tariff and energy policies or de-globalization trends: factors that are not specific to the industry. Certain aspects of the regulatory realm, like Europe’s preference for codes that clad mass timber, are examples of how mass timber industries can reduce shared, intermediating forces in order to generate and partially retain value currently unrealized.
